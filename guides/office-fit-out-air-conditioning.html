How do you plan air conditioning for an office fit out?

Reviewed November 2026 by Zac Hancox, Founder of CoolIn

The short answer

Bring the air conditioning in at the design stage, before the layout and ceiling are fixed, because the pipework has to go in the ceiling void before the ceilings close. Check what the landlord's base build already provides, size each zone to its real heat load, and budget from around £8,500 plus VAT for a four cassette open plan floor.

  • Pipework goes in at first fix, before the ceiling grid goes up
  • A Cat A system can often be adapted to a Cat B layout without moving the outdoor plant
  • Meeting rooms, glazed elevations and the comms room each need their own zone
  • Most leases need written landlord consent before plant is fixed or walls are cored
  • Air conditioning recirculates room air, so fresh air needs planning separately
  • Four cassette open plan floor around £8,500, six unit VRF around £14,000, plus VAT

An office fit out is the cheapest time to get the air conditioning right. The ceilings are open, the layout is still on paper and every trade is on site anyway. Get it wrong now and you're lifting ceiling tiles and moving units six months after you've moved in.

This guide is for the business taking the space: the tenant moving floors, the owner refurbishing, or the office manager handed the project. If you're a contractor pricing the air conditioning package on someone else's fit out, the contractor page covers tenders, builder's work and handover.

When should air conditioning be planned in an office fit out?

At the design stage, alongside the layout. Refrigerant pipework, condensate drains and control cables all run in the ceiling void, so they have to go in at first fix, before the ceiling grid goes up. The indoor units then go in at second fix, after the ceilings and decoration.

If the air conditioning is left until the partitions are up, the usual result is units in the wrong places, ceilings opened twice and a meeting room that's too hot from the first week. The layout and the cooling are one decision, not two.

What is the difference between Cat A and Cat B for air conditioning?

Cat A is the landlord's base build. The floor is handed over open plan with a raised floor, a suspended ceiling, lighting and usually a base air conditioning system, often cassettes or VRF laid out evenly for whichever tenant arrives.

Cat B is the tenant's fit out. Meeting rooms, partitions, kitchens and a comms room go in, and the air conditioning has to follow them. Units move to sit inside the new rooms, zones are rebalanced and the comms room gets its own cooling.

Adapting a Cat A system to a Cat B layout is often cheaper than it looks, because the outdoor plant and the main pipework usually stay where they are.

What should you check with the landlord first?

Before anything is designed, ask for:

  • The existing air conditioning layout, the equipment schedule and its age
  • The service history and the F-Gas records, which show whether it's been looked after
  • The spare capacity on the outdoor plant, if any
  • Where new condensers are allowed to go, and the rules on roof access
  • The landlord's consent process and what they need to see
  • Whether the lease requires you to strip the system out and make good when you leave
  • The date of the last TM44 inspection, if the system is over 12kW

The reinstatement clause is the one tenants miss. If the lease requires you to remove the system at the end, that's a cost to price now rather than discover at exit.

How do you design the air conditioning around the layout?

Size each area to its real heat load, not a square metre rule of thumb. Four things push the load up in an office:

  • Glazing. A south or west facing elevation can run several degrees warmer than the rooms behind it, so it needs its own zone.
  • Meeting rooms. A room of twelve people with the door shut heats up quickly. Meeting rooms need cooling sized for when they're full, not the average day.
  • Equipment. Monitors, printers and kitchen appliances all add heat. In a small office they can be a meaningful share of the total.
  • The comms room. A switch and firewall run around the clock, so the comms room needs dedicated cooling that runs when the rest of the office is off.

Then set zones that match how people sit. Open plan usually shares control across several units, while meeting rooms, glazed corners and private offices get their own. Locking the temperature range stops anyone driving it down to 16 degrees.

Which air conditioning system suits an office fit out?

The ceiling decides most of it.

SystemBest forNotes
Ceiling cassetteSuspended ceilings and open planSits flush in the grid and needs around 300mm of void
Slim ductedDesigned fit outs and clean ceilingsThe unit hides in the void and feeds linear grilles
VRF or VRVSix or more indoor units, or several floorsHeat recovery moves heat from the sunny side to the shaded side
Wall mountedSmall offices and period buildings with no voidCheapest per room

Exposed ceilings, common in Manchester's converted mills and warehouses, rule out cassettes. There, the choice is usually exposed ducted units with a designed duct run, or wall units placed carefully on the solid walls.

Does office air conditioning provide fresh air?

Not on its own. Most systems recirculate and cool the air already in the room, so a sealed meeting room can be cool and still feel stuffy. Offices also need fresh air, from openable windows, a ventilation system or heat recovery units that bring outside air in while keeping most of the heat or cooling.

A fit out is the time to plan both together, because they share the same ceiling void. Heat recovery ventilation for an office floor costs from around £5,200 plus VAT. The ventilation page explains the options.

A four cassette open plan floor is typically three to five days on site across first and second fix. Work in an occupied building can be done in the evenings and at weekends at standard labour rates.

What should you get at handover?

  • Commissioning sheets for every unit
  • The F-Gas record with the refrigerant charge and CO2 equivalent
  • Electrical certificates
  • Operation and maintenance manuals
  • The manufacturer's warranty registered
  • A walk through of the controls for whoever runs the office

Book the first service at handover too. Annual servicing is usually a condition of the warranty, and systems over the F-Gas threshold need leak checks recorded. The servicing page sets out the plans.

Questions people ask next

Can we reuse the air conditioning that is already in the building?

Often, yes. On a Cat A floor the outdoor plant and main pipework can usually stay, and indoor units are moved or added to suit your layout. It depends on the age of the system, its refrigerant, whether it has been serviced and whether it has the capacity for your meeting rooms and equipment. A survey of the existing plant answers that before you design around it.

Who pays for air conditioning in an office fit out, the landlord or the tenant?

It depends on the lease and the category of fit out. A Cat A floor usually comes with the base system paid for by the landlord, and the tenant pays to adapt it in a Cat B fit out. Check the lease for who owns the plant, who maintains it and whether you have to remove it when you leave.

Do we need landlord consent to install air conditioning?

Usually, yes. Most office leases need written consent before plant is fixed to the structure, a core is drilled through an external wall or a condenser goes on the roof. An equipment schedule with condenser positions and pipe routes is normally what a landlord wants to see.

How long does office air conditioning take to install during a fit out?

A four cassette open plan floor is typically three to five days on site including commissioning, split between first fix before the ceilings close and second fix after. Larger VRF systems are programmed floor by floor around the rest of the fit out.

Does office air conditioning provide fresh air?

Not on its own. Most air conditioning recirculates and cools the air already in the room. Offices also need fresh air, which comes from openable windows, a ventilation system or heat recovery units, so plan the two together during the fit out.

Do we need a TM44 inspection after the fit out?

If the air conditioning in the building is over 12kW of effective rated output, yes, at least every five years. Four 3.5kW cassettes already cross that line. The duty sits with whoever controls the system, which after a Cat B fit out may be the tenant.
