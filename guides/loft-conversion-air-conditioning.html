Can you put air conditioning in a loft conversion?

Reviewed November 2026 by Zac Hancox, Founder of CoolIn

The short answer

Yes, and a loft conversion is often the room that needs it most. A wall mounted unit or a low level console goes in the room, and the outdoor unit sits on a flat dormer roof, a rear wall or the ground. Lofts often need a slightly bigger unit than the floor area suggests, because of the heat coming through the roof.

  • Lofts overheat first, because heat rises and the roof takes the sun all day
  • Often sized one step up from the floor area, because of the roof and roof windows
  • A flat dormer roof can usually take the outdoor unit, at least a metre from the edge
  • The outdoor unit can't go on a pitched roof under permitted development
  • Usually fitted in one day, with the pipes run down the outside in a slim white cover
  • One room from £1,650 fitted, with no VAT to add until 31 March 2027

A loft conversion is usually the best room in the house for nine months of the year and the worst for three. Come June, the room you spent money converting is too hot to sleep in by mid evening. Fitted air conditioning fixes that, and it heats the room in winter too.

Why do loft conversions get so hot?

Three things work against a loft at once:

  • Heat rises. Warm air from every room below collects at the top of the house.
  • The roof is in the sun all day. A slate or tile roof heats up fast, and that heat comes straight through the sloping ceiling.
  • Roof windows and dormers let the sun in. A south or west facing roof window puts a lot of heat into a small room.

Insulation slows the heat coming in during the day, but it also holds it in at night, which is why lofts stay hot long after the sun has gone down. Older conversions, with thinner insulation, are the worst.

What size air conditioning does a loft need?

Often one step up from what the floor area alone suggests, because of the roof and the glass. A loft bedroom that would take a 2.5kW unit downstairs might need 3.5kW, especially with a south facing roof window. The size calculator gives you a starting figure in under a minute, and the survey confirms it.

Where does the indoor unit go?

Sloping ceilings limit where a wall unit can sit, so there are three usual options:

  • A wall mounted unit on the gable end wall or the front wall of a dormer, wherever there's enough straight wall high up. The most common and the most efficient.
  • A low level floor console under the eaves, on the short knee wall. Ideal when there isn't enough straight wall for a high unit, and good at heating because it blows warm air in at floor level.
  • A ducted unit hidden in the eaves, behind the knee wall, with only small grilles showing. The neatest finish, and one ducted unit can serve two rooms.

Where does the outdoor unit go?

The rules are the same as for any house, with one point that matters more in a loft:

  • A flat dormer roof can usually take the outdoor unit, as long as it's at least a metre from the edge. It keeps the pipe run short.
  • The rear wall, on brackets, or on the ground at the back, with the pipes run down the wall.
  • Not on a pitched roof. Permitted development doesn't cover a unit on a pitched roof, so that would need a planning application.

On a house, one outdoor unit is normally allowed under permitted development if it meets the size, position and noise rules. Conservation areas and listed buildings add restrictions. The planning permission article sets out every condition.

How do the pipes get from the loft to the outdoor unit?

Through one neat hole in the gable or rear wall, then down the outside in a slim white cover to the outdoor unit. Where there's a void behind the knee wall, the pipes can often run through the eaves instead, out of sight. The drain for the condensate runs to a gutter or drain outside, or a small pump lifts it where gravity won't work.

Our installed prices include up to five metres of pipe, which covers most lofts with the outdoor unit on a dormer roof. A unit on the ground two floors down can need a longer run, which we price at the survey.

Will it heat the loft in winter too?

Yes. Lofts get cold in winter for the same reasons they get hot in summer, and the same unit heats the room as an air to air heat pump. It gives several units of heat for each unit of electricity, so it's far cheaper to run than a plug in heater. The article on heating with air conditioning covers the best settings.

Questions people ask next

Can the outdoor unit go on the roof?

Not on a pitched roof under permitted development. On a flat roof, such as the top of a dormer, it can, as long as it sits at least a metre from the edge and meets the noise limit. If the only workable position is a pitched roof, it needs a planning application.

Will air conditioning heat my loft in winter?

Yes. Lofts get cold in winter for the same reason they get hot in summer, and the same unit heats the room as an air to air heat pump, far more cheaply than a plug in heater.

Is it quiet enough for a bedroom?

Yes. The noisy part sits outside. Quiet indoor units start around 19 dB(A) on their lowest setting, about the level of a whisper, and most have a night mode that turns the fan down further.

Can one outdoor unit cool the loft and other rooms?

Yes. A multi split system runs two to five indoor units from one outdoor unit, so the loft and the bedrooms below can each have their own unit and their own control, from £3,450 fitted.

How long does it take to fit?

A single room is usually fitted in one day. Longer pipe runs down from the loft, or a ducted system hidden in the eaves, can take a little longer, and the survey tells you the exact time.
