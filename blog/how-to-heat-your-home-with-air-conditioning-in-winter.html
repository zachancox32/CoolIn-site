How to heat your home with air conditioning this winter

11 November 2026 · 4 min read · By Zac Hancox, Founder of CoolIn

If you've got air conditioning fitted, you've also got a heater, and this winter it's one of the cheapest ways to warm a room. A wall mounted unit in heat mode works as an air to air heat pump: it moves warmth from the air outside into the room, even on a cold day. Plenty of people only ever press the snowflake, which leaves half of what they paid for unused.

Does air conditioning really heat a room?

Yes. Set the remote to heat mode, usually shown as a sun symbol, and the system runs in reverse. The outdoor unit collects heat from the outside air and the indoor unit blows it into the room. It works well into the winter, and a current unit keeps heating at temperatures well below freezing, with less output as it gets colder.

Is it cheaper than gas?

Per unit of heat, at this winter's prices, yes. At the Ofgem price cap for October to December 2026, electricity is 26.32p per kWh, with no VAT on electricity until 31 March 2027, and gas is 7.97p per kWh. A current air conditioner gives around four and a half units of heat for every unit of electricity it uses over a season.

Efficiency falls on the coldest days, though, so in a hard frost the gap narrows and a boiler can come out ahead. The price cap also changes on 1 January, so the figures will move. The running costs article shows the working so you can put your own tariff in, and the statistics page is updated each time the cap changes.

The real saving usually comes from heating only the rooms you're using. The comparison with a gas boiler goes through where each one wins.

The best settings for heating

  • Use heat mode, not auto. Auto can switch to cooling on a sunny afternoon and back again, which wastes energy.
  • Set 19 to 21°C and leave it. A modern unit is most efficient when it's holding a steady temperature. Turning it off and on means it has to work flat out every time to catch up.
  • Don't set it to 30°C to warm up faster. It won't heat any quicker. It'll just overshoot and cost more.
  • Point the louvres down. Warm air rises, and a wall unit sits high, so aim the airflow at the floor.
  • Leave the fan on auto, or low in a bedroom if you'd rather it was quieter.
  • Use the timer. Start it half an hour before you need the room, so it's warm when you walk in.
  • Close the door. Heating one room is cheap. Heating the landing as well isn't.

Why does it stop blowing warm air for a few minutes?

That's the defrost cycle, and it's normal. In cold, damp weather, which is most of a Manchester winter, frost builds up on the outdoor unit. Every so often the system pauses heating for a few minutes to melt it off. You might see steam rising from the outdoor unit, and the indoor fan may stop. Then it carries on as before.

If it happens constantly, or ice keeps building up on the outdoor unit, ring us, because something isn't right.

Look after the outdoor unit

  • Keep it clear. Leaves, snow and anything stored against it cut the airflow and make it work harder.
  • Never cover it. A tarpaulin over the top stops it working altogether.
  • Watch the ground underneath. Defrost water drips from the unit and can freeze on a path, so keep it clear or grit it.

Clean the filters

Behind the front panel of the indoor unit are two mesh filters. In a winter of heavy use, rinse them every few weeks under the tap, let them dry and slide them back. Dusty filters cut the heat output noticeably. Everything else, including the coils and the refrigerant checks, is part of an annual service.

Using it alongside your boiler

You don't have to choose. Many people use the air conditioning for the rooms they're in most, a home office in the day or the lounge in the evening, and turn the radiator down in that room. The boiler still does the whole house in a proper cold snap and makes the hot water.

When should you call us?

  • No warm air in heat mode after fifteen minutes, and it isn't defrosting
  • Water dripping from the indoor unit
  • An error code or flashing light on the indoor unit
  • Ice that stays on the outdoor unit
